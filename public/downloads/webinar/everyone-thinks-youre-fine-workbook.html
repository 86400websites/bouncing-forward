Stage three

Imagine

Imagine isn’t about getting life back to how it was. It’s the next chapter, with a load you can actually live with.

21One honest direction

Something like: “I’d like to keep doing what matters for my family, and still have one evening a week that’s mine.” Then check whose face comes to mind. A picture that lightens your load has your own face in it.

22My repeatable week

An ordinary week you could do again and again without running out of yourself. Five small moves, and one of each is plenty. None needs money, only a decision and a conversation.

23What’s still good?

Real optimism has nothing to do with forced cheerfulness. It’s noticing the laugh around the table, the voice note from a grandchild, the friend who says thank you.

Stage four

Action

For people who hold it all together, action rarely means doing more. The bravest action is usually subtraction: cancel, delegate, delay, decline, ask.

24My 24-hour move

Ten minutes or less, starting with an action word, done before this time tomorrow. Giving it a “when” is what lets your mind close the tab.

25Delegate, or ask for support?

Delegating hands something over completely, the task and the deciding. Support means it stays yours, but you stop carrying it alone. If handing over feels too big, start with support.

26My clear ask

“Shout if you need anything” rarely turns into help. A clear ask gives people something they can actually do: what, by when, and for how long.

27My handover note

If you were away for a week, would anyone know how? Half a page turns “nobody else can do it properly” into “here’s how”. The nerves usually leave after the ask, rather than before it.

28As life changes

A new grandchild, a new manager, a parent who needs a little more help. The road changes; you change the route and keep the destination. That’s adaptability.

29“Let me check and come back to you.”

One sentence that stops the load building quite so fast. A moment to decide, rather than a reflex yes.

The webinar in two pages

Everything we covered, in the order we covered it.

How many tabs are open?

Many of the tabs in your head are invisible: remembering, deciding, worrying ahead, keeping the peace. Most of us are worn down by the load we carry in our heads as much as by the tasks.

Everyone thinks you’re fine

From the outside it all looks under control, so more keeps coming your way: one more lift, one more committee, one more “Could you just…”. The people who struggle most can often cope with almost anything.

Two kinds of loss

The time, sleep and weekends that used to be yours, and the life you had pictured for this stage. You can love the people in your life completely and still miss that life. Both are true at once.

Replay yesterday

List everything you did, remembered, decided or worried about for someone else, and circle the three that cost the most energy. What drains us most is rarely the biggest job. It’s the one we never chose, and never put down.

Better organising adds no deposits

Every demand is a withdrawal from your capacity account, and recovery is a deposit. A better list only rearranges the withdrawals. Deciding when something will happen is what lets your mind close the tab.

Reduce the load

We would never praise a bridge for carrying twice its safe load. We would reduce the load. “I should be able to manage all of this” turns a question about the load into a judgement about you.

A limit is not a lack of care

A limit is what keeps your care possible over time, for the people who need you, and for you. Accepting what can’t change frees your energy for what you can shape.

Fixed or only familiar

When we’re tired, we treat far more of life as fixed than it really is. The Saturday shop, every birthday present, replying straight away: much of what feels fixed is only familiar, and familiar can move.

Guilt sounds like love

Real responsibility sits on one list, and what guilt has added sits on another. “They’ll think I don’t care” is guilt talking, and research shows people are far less hurt by a no than we expect. Kindness is a choice; guilt is a pressure.

Resilience includes recovery

Like bamboo, real resilience bends and then straightens up again. Recovery is part of strength: the early night, the walk, and the cup of tea you finish while it’s still hot.

Support needs to see the load

Support can only lighten a load it can see, and people who hold it all together are experts at editing what they tell. Start small: one task, one person, the unedited version.

Column one is shorter than you think

Only I can do this, someone else could do this, this could wait or stop. Column one is usually shorter than you expect, and some of it may be something you have quietly made yours.

A repeatable week

You don’t need an ideal life. You need a week you can repeat without running out of yourself. Stop one thing, protect one block of time, share one, lower one standard, leave one space empty.

Action is subtraction

For people who hold it all together, action rarely means doing more. Cancel, delegate, delay, decline or ask, with one move of ten minutes or less before this time tomorrow.

A clear ask and a handover note

“Shout if you need anything” rarely turns into help, and a clear ask does. Half a page of how-to turns “nobody else can do it properly” into “here’s how”, and most people have been waiting to be asked.

Optimism and adaptability

Real optimism notices what’s still good. Adaptability changes the route and keeps the destination: as life changes, the way the load is shared changes too. “Let me check and come back to you.”

Your road map

The path is the steps you take: Honesty, Accept, Reflect, Imagine and Action. The compass holds the strengths you lean on along the way: resilience, support, optimism and adaptability. Every one of them was yours before this hour, and it is still yours today.
